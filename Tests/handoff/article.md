# Why the tide comes twice a day

By A. Mariner, 1 March 2026

Anyone who has kept a boat in a harbour knows that the water comes up the wall twice between one noon and the next, and most people who are asked why will say that the moon pulls it. That is half of the answer, and the half that is easy to believe.

## The pull of the moon

The moon pulls on everything on the earth, and most strongly on what is nearest to it. The sea on the side that faces the moon is drawn towards it and heaps up a little, a rise of well under a metre in the open ocean, which a shelving coast and a narrowing channel can turn into several metres at a harbour wall.

The far bulge is the part people find hard to believe.

> Time and tide wait for none.

On the side of the earth away from the moon the sea is pulled least of all, less than the solid earth under it, so the earth is drawn away from the water and the water is left standing higher there too. A harbour is carried through both heaps as the earth turns, which is two high waters a day, a little under twelve and a half hours apart.

## Springs and neaps

The sun does the same as the moon at a little under half the strength. When the two pull along one line, at the new and the full moon, the heaps are at their highest and the hollows between them at their lowest, and those are the spring tides; at the quarters they pull across each other, and the range is at its least.

- Springs: a day or two after the new and the full moon.

- Neaps: a day or two after the first and the last quarter.
